Philosophy

I hold trades for a few days to a few weeks. I don't day trade. I'll take a position when the chart looks right and the news around the stock supports it, sometimes with leverage.

For options I lean toward contracts with a lot of vega, so if volatility picks up I make money on that even if the stock hasn't moved much yet. I also sell premium against what I'm holding. That money gets put back into the next trade, and over time that's what grows the account.

Record

This approach gave me about two and a half years of 100% portfolio growth. Then I ran more leverage than I should have and the account was liquidated. The ideas were good; the risk management wasn't. That's the reason for the hiatus. Before I get back in I'm learning risk management properly: how much to size, how much leverage, and where I'm out, decided before the trade and not during it.

Background

Over the past couple of years I developed a deep interest in human biology and pharmacology — not from a clinical angle, but from a systems perspective. Understanding how the body works at a mechanistic level, what the levers are, and how to pull them intentionally.

Pharmacology and Performance

Most of what people call performance comes down to a few things: how well you sleep, how fast you recover, where your hormones are at, and how much inflammation you're carrying around. Pharmacology is interesting to me because it acts on those directly. Better sleep means a better next day. Faster recovery means you can train more. Being more awake means more useful hours.

What I find most interesting is the trade-offs. Something can work and still not be worth it once you count the side effects, the tolerance, and what happens when you stop. I treat it the way I'd treat any system: understand the mechanism first, measure one variable at a time, and be honest about what the data says.

Case Study

I'm interested in building a comparative case study of CEOs and business owners who leverage modern compounds and research — peptides, nootropics, biohacking protocols — and measuring how their companies have grown relative to peers who haven't. The hypothesis: the edge compounds at the top.

Why

Take two identical companies. Same product, same market, same headcount. The only difference is that the people running group A sleep better, recover faster, and have more good hours in a day because they use modern medicine and the tools that come with it, and group B doesn't. Over a quarter you probably can't tell them apart. Over five years I think you can, and I want to know by how much.

If the gap is real, it raises a few questions. Should a company have someone in house for this the same way it has someone for IT or legal? Could it be offered as a coaching program instead? And what would it actually cost compared to what it returns? Those are the questions the case study is meant to answer.
